## Settling In

Hello, and welcome. Tonight, we are going to drift slowly through one hundred quiet facts about the universe. From our own star, out past the planets, through the lives of distant suns, and into the deep, dark spaces between the galaxies.

If you enjoy these calm journeys, a like or a subscribe helps other sleepy minds find their way here. And if you would like, tell me in the comments where you are listening from tonight, and what time it is for you. It is always lovely to know who is drifting off with us.

Now, dim the lights. Let your shoulders drop. You do not need to remember anything at all. If you fall asleep halfway through, that just means it worked.

Let us begin, close to home, with the Sun.

## Our Star

Fact one. The Sun holds almost all of the mass in our solar system. About ninety nine point eight percent of it. Every planet, every moon, every asteroid and comet, all of them together, make up only the tiniest remainder. In a very real sense, the solar system is the Sun, with a few small crumbs circling around it.

Fact two. You could fit about one point three million Earths inside the Sun. If the Sun were a hollow ball, and you poured Earths into it like marbles, you would be pouring for a very, very long time. And still, among the stars of our galaxy, the Sun is a fairly ordinary size.

Fact three. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. So when you feel the warmth of the Sun on your face, that light left the Sun a little over eight minutes earlier. If the Sun were to vanish, quietly, we would not know for another eight minutes. The light already on its way would keep arriving, as if nothing had changed.

Fact four. The light itself is far older than that. Energy made deep in the Sun's core does not travel straight out. It wanders, bouncing from particle to particle, in a slow, crowded journey through the Sun's dense interior. Estimates vary widely, but that journey may take tens of thousands of years, or far longer. Only then does it escape, and cross the gap to Earth in just eight minutes.

Fact five. The Sun's core is about fifteen million degrees Celsius. Its visible surface is far cooler, around five and a half thousand degrees. But strangely, the thin outer atmosphere of the Sun, called the corona, is over a million degrees. Hotter than the surface beneath it. Exactly how the corona stays so hot is still one of the Sun's quiet mysteries.

Fact six. Every second, the Sun turns about four million tonnes of matter into energy. It does this through fusion, pressing hydrogen together into helium in its core. Four million tonnes, every single second. And yet the Sun is so enormous that it has been doing this for billions of years, and has billions of years left to go.

Fact seven. The Sun is about four point six billion years old. That puts it roughly halfway through its long, steady middle age. It has been shining calmly since before the Earth had oceans, and it will keep shining for billions of years more. There is no hurry at all.

Fact eight. Sunspots look dark, but they are not. They are simply cooler patches on the Sun's surface, where tangled magnetic fields hold back some of the heat. A sunspot is still thousands of degrees, and if you could see one on its own, it would glow brightly. It only looks dark beside the even brighter surface around it. The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of about eleven years.

Fact nine. The Sun is always breathing out a gentle stream of particles, called the solar wind. It flows outward in every direction, past all the planets. When some of those particles meet Earth's magnetic field, they are guided down toward the poles, where they make the air glow. Those soft curtains of green and violet light are the auroras.

Fact ten. The Sun is travelling around the centre of our galaxy, and one full orbit takes roughly two hundred and thirty million years. The last time the Sun was in this part of its orbit, the very first dinosaurs were only just beginning to appear. One slow lap of the galaxy, while all of Earth's long history unfolded below.

## The Inner Worlds

Fact eleven. On Mercury, a year is shorter than a day. Mercury circles the Sun in just eighty eight Earth days. But it turns so slowly, and in such a particular rhythm with its orbit, that from sunrise to sunrise takes about one hundred and seventy six Earth days. Two full years for a single day. Breakfast would be a long, drawn out affair.

Fact twelve. Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun, and yet it has ice. Near its poles are craters so deep that sunlight never reaches their floors. In those permanent shadows, a spacecraft found evidence of water ice. Frost, hidden on the world closest to the fire.

Fact thirteen. Venus spins backwards. Compared with most of the planets, it turns the opposite way. So on Venus, the Sun would rise in the west, and set in the east. And it turns so slowly that one rotation takes about two hundred and forty three Earth days. That is longer than its whole year around the Sun.

Fact fourteen. Venus is the hottest planet, hotter even than Mercury. Its surface is around four hundred and sixty five degrees Celsius, hot enough to melt lead. Its thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide traps the heat like a heavy blanket that never comes off. The pressure at the surface is about ninety times that of Earth, like being nearly a kilometre deep in the ocean.

Fact fifteen. After the Moon, Venus is the brightest natural object in our night sky. It shines so steadily near sunrise and sunset that people have long called it the morning star, and the evening star. It is not a star at all, of course. It is a world wrapped in bright clouds, catching the light of the Sun.

Fact sixteen. Earth is the only place we know of where life exists. About seventy one percent of its surface is covered by water. From space, it is mostly blue, swirled with white clouds. A small, wet, living world, turning quietly in the light.

Fact seventeen. The Earth is not perfectly round. Its spin makes it bulge a little at the middle. The distance across the Earth at the equator is about forty three kilometres more than the distance from pole to pole. It is slightly wider than it is tall, like someone who has just finished a very satisfying dinner.

Fact eighteen. Mars is red because it is rusty. Its dust and rocks are rich in iron oxide, the same compound that turns old nails and garden gates reddish brown. Winds carry that fine dust across the whole planet, and sometimes into storms that can wrap the entire world in a dusty haze.

Fact nineteen. Mars has the tallest volcano known in the solar system. It is called Olympus Mons, and it rises about twenty two kilometres high, around two and a half times the height of Mount Everest. Mars also has a canyon system, Valles Marineris, that stretches for about four thousand kilometres. Long enough to reach across much of a continent.

Fact twenty. Sunsets on Mars are blue. The fine dust in the Martian air scatters light differently from the air on Earth. Near the setting Sun, the sky takes on a cool, soft blue glow, while the rest of the sky stays a dusty butterscotch. A reversed sunset, on a quiet, faraway world.

## Giants and Their Moons

Fact twenty one. Jupiter is more than twice as massive as all the other planets in the solar system put together. It is a vast world of gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface to stand on. If you tried to land, you would simply sink, deeper and deeper, into ever thicker clouds.

Fact twenty two. Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm larger than the Earth. People have been watching it for well over a century and a half. In recent decades it has been slowly shrinking, but it still churns on, a great swirling eye in Jupiter's clouds, older than anyone alive today.

Fact twenty three. The largest moon in the solar system orbits Jupiter. It is called Ganymede, and it is bigger than the planet Mercury. It even has its own magnetic field, which is something no other moon is known to have.

Fact twenty four. Another of Jupiter's moons, Europa, is covered in a shell of ice. And beneath that ice, scientists believe there is a deep, salty ocean. It may hold more water than all of Earth's oceans combined. A hidden sea, in the dark, under a frozen sky.

Fact twenty five. Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active place we know of. It has hundreds of volcanoes, some of them throwing plumes high above its surface. Io is squeezed and stretched by Jupiter's gravity as it orbits, and that constant kneading heats its insides. A restless little world, never quite able to settle down.

Fact twenty six. Saturn is less dense than water. It is so light for its size that, if you could find a bathtub large enough, Saturn would float. Finding the bathtub would be the difficult part. And you would probably want to clean the ring off afterwards.

Fact twenty seven. Saturn's rings are mostly made of water ice, from tiny grains to chunks the size of houses. The main rings stretch around two hundred and eighty thousand kilometres from edge to edge. But in many places they are only tens of metres thick. Wider than most of the distance to the Moon, and in places, thinner than a city block is tall.

Fact twenty eight. Saturn's largest moon, Titan, has a thick, hazy atmosphere, and lakes and seas on its surface. But they are not filled with water. They are filled with liquid methane and ethane. On Titan, it rains, slowly, gently, drops of natural gas falling from orange skies.

Fact twenty nine. Uranus rolls around the Sun on its side. Its spin axis is tipped over by about ninety eight degrees. Because of this, each of its poles spends around forty two years in continuous sunlight, followed by about forty two years of darkness. A very long summer, and a very long night.

Fact thirty. Neptune has the fastest winds in the solar system, reaching around two thousand kilometres per hour. It was also the first planet found by mathematics before it was seen. In the eighteen forties, astronomers noticed Uranus was being tugged slightly off course, calculated where an unseen planet should be, and pointed a telescope there. And there it was.

## The Far Edges

Fact thirty one. Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet in two thousand and six. But it did not seem to mind. When a spacecraft flew past it in two thousand and fifteen, it revealed a world with mountains of ice, and a huge, bright, heart shaped plain on its surface. Small, distant, and quietly lovely.

Fact thirty two. One year on Pluto lasts about two hundred and forty eight Earth years. It was discovered in nineteen thirty, and since then it has not yet completed a single trip around the Sun. Its first birthday since being found will not arrive until the late twenty one hundreds.

Fact thirty three. Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a wide ring of icy bodies, left over from when the solar system formed. Pluto lives there, along with countless frozen worlds, large and small. Leftover building blocks, drifting slowly in the cold.

Fact thirty four. Even farther out, there may be a vast, faint shell of icy objects surrounding the whole solar system. It is called the Oort Cloud. No one has ever seen it directly. But many long period comets seem to come from there, falling inward from the deep dark, and it may stretch more than a light year from the Sun.

Fact thirty five. The farthest human made object is a spacecraft called Voyager 1. It was launched in nineteen seventy seven, and it is now more than twenty four billion kilometres away, travelling through interstellar space. A radio message from Earth now takes nearly a full day to reach it. Still going, still quietly listening.

Fact thirty six. Both Voyager spacecraft carry a golden record. On it are sounds and images from Earth. Greetings in dozens of languages, music from many cultures, the sound of rain, of wind, of a mother's kiss. A small message in a bottle, cast into the cosmic ocean.

Fact thirty seven. A comet's tail always points away from the Sun. As a comet comes closer to the Sun, its ice begins to turn to gas, and the solar wind and sunlight push that gas and dust outward. So when a comet travels back out into the dark, it leaves tail first.

Fact thirty eight. Halley's Comet returns to the inner solar system about every seventy six years. Its last visit was in nineteen eighty six, and the next is expected in twenty sixty one. For many people, it is a once in a lifetime visitor. A slow, faithful traveller, keeping its long appointment with the Sun.

Fact thirty nine. The asteroid belt is mostly empty space. In films, it is often shown as a crowded field of tumbling rocks. In reality, asteroids are usually hundreds of thousands of kilometres apart. And all the asteroids in the belt together add up to only a few percent of the mass of our Moon.

Fact forty. The largest object in the asteroid belt is Ceres, a small dwarf planet. On its surface, a spacecraft found bright, glowing spots inside a crater. They turned out to be deposits of salt, left behind as salty water seeped up and slowly evaporated. Pale salt flats, shining on a tiny, dark world.

## The Stars

Fact forty one. Stars twinkle because of our air. Their light travels across space in a steady stream, but on its last journey through Earth's atmosphere, it is bent and wobbled by shifting layers of warm and cool air. Planets usually twinkle less, because they appear as tiny discs rather than single points of light.

Fact forty two. The colour of a star tells you its temperature. Red stars are cooler, around three thousand degrees. Yellow stars, like our Sun, are in the middle. And blue white stars are the hottest, often more than ten thousand degrees. The night sky is quietly colour coded, if you look closely.

Fact forty three. The nearest star to the Sun is Proxima Centauri, about four point two light years away. That is around forty trillion kilometres. Voyager 1, travelling at its present speed, would take more than seventy thousand years to cover a distance like that. Our nearest neighbour is still very, very far.

Fact forty four. Most stars in our galaxy are small, dim red dwarfs. They are so cool and faint that not a single one is visible to the naked eye. Red dwarfs burn their fuel so slowly that they may shine for trillions of years. The universe is not yet old enough for any of them to have died of old age.

Fact forty five. Betelgeuse, the reddish star in the shoulder of Orion, is a red supergiant. It is so enormous that if it replaced our Sun, its surface would reach past the orbit of Mars. A few years ago, it dimmed noticeably, and people wondered if it was about to explode. It turned out to have puffed out a cloud of dust. Even giants need to clear their throats.

Fact forty six. Many stars share their lives with a partner. Our Sun travels alone, but a large fraction of stars like it have a companion, and some live in groups of three or more. On a planet around such stars, there might be two suns in the sky, rising and setting together.

Fact forty seven. Polaris, the North Star, is not the brightest star in the sky. It is only around the fiftieth brightest. Its fame comes from where it sits, almost directly above Earth's north pole. And because Earth's axis slowly wobbles over about twenty six thousand years, Polaris will not always be the pole star. In about twelve thousand years, that role will pass to the bright star Vega.

Fact forty eight. When you look at Polaris, the light reaching your eyes left it around four centuries ago. It set off at roughly the time the first telescopes were being pointed at the sky. Every star you see is a message from the past, arriving gently, right on time.

Fact forty nine. By many estimates, there are more stars in the observable universe than grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches. The numbers are so large that they stop meaning very much. So perhaps just picture a beach at night, the sand stretching away in the dark, and the sky above it, fuller still.

Fact fifty. On a clear, dark night far from city lights, you can see a few thousand stars with your own eyes. That is only a tiny sliver of the stars in our own galaxy, the ones close enough and bright enough to reach you. The rest are there too, beyond sight, shining quietly.

## Lives of the Stars

Fact fifty one. Stars are born inside vast clouds of gas and dust, called nebulae. Over time, parts of a cloud slowly collapse under their own gravity, growing denser and hotter, until fusion begins and a new star switches on. In the Eagle Nebula, towering columns of gas known as the Pillars of Creation are a nursery for newborn stars, light years tall.

Fact fifty two. Stars shine by fusion. Deep in their cores, the pressure and heat are so great that hydrogen nuclei are squeezed together to make helium. Each time, a little mass is turned into energy. That energy is what holds a star up against its own weight, and what pours out as starlight.

Fact fifty three. Much of you was made inside stars. The carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones, and the iron in your blood were forged in stars that lived and died long before the Sun was born. The hydrogen in you is even older, made in the very early universe. You are, quite literally, made from the ashes of ancient stars.

Fact fifty four. In about five billion years, the Sun will swell into a red giant. It will grow enormously, possibly swallowing Mercury and Venus. Later, it will gently shed its outer layers into space, leaving behind a small, hot, glowing core called a white dwarf. A long, slow ending, still unimaginably far away.

Fact fifty five. A white dwarf packs something like the mass of the Sun into a ball about the size of the Earth. Its material is so dense that a single teaspoon of it would weigh several tonnes. And it has no fuel left to burn. It simply glows, slowly cooling, over billions upon billions of years.

Fact fifty six. When a much larger star reaches the end of its life, it can explode in a supernova. For a few weeks, a single exploding star can shine as brightly as an entire galaxy of billions of stars. Those explosions scatter new elements out into space, seeds for future stars and planets.

Fact fifty seven. In the year one thousand and fifty four, astronomers in China recorded a new star in the sky. It was bright enough to be seen in daylight for weeks. Today, at that spot, we see the Crab Nebula, the glowing, expanding remains of that supernova. A thousand year old echo, still spreading out.

Fact fifty eight. Sometimes, a supernova leaves behind a neutron star. It may have more mass than our Sun, squeezed into a ball only about twenty kilometres across. A teaspoon of it would weigh around a billion tonnes. Some neutron stars spin hundreds of times every second.

Fact fifty nine. In nineteen sixty seven, a young astronomer named Jocelyn Bell Burnell noticed a strange, perfectly regular radio pulse coming from space. It was so steady that, half jokingly, the signal was nicknamed Little Green Men. It turned out to be a spinning neutron star, sweeping a beam past Earth like a lighthouse. The first pulsar ever found.

Fact sixty. A black hole is a place where gravity is so strong that not even light can escape. For a long time, no one had ever seen one. Then, in twenty nineteen, a network of telescopes around the world captured the first image of a black hole's shadow, a dark circle ringed by glowing gas, in a distant galaxy. A few years later, they imaged the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy too.

## The Galaxies

Fact sixty one. Our galaxy, the Milky Way, is roughly a hundred thousand light years across. It holds somewhere between one hundred and four hundred billion stars. On a very dark night, the faint band of light stretching across the sky is our own galaxy, seen from the inside.

Fact sixty two. We live about twenty six thousand light years from the centre of the galaxy, in one of its smaller spiral arms. It is a fairly quiet neighbourhood. Not too crowded. Not too close to the busy, bright middle. A good place to settle down, as it turns out.

Fact sixty three. At the centre of the Milky Way is a supermassive black hole, called Sagittarius A star. It has about four million times the mass of the Sun. And yet, compared with the galaxy around it, it is very small. Stars circle it, slowly, for millions of years.

Fact sixty four. The Andromeda Galaxy is about two and a half million light years away. On a very dark night, it can be seen without a telescope as a faint, soft smudge. It is one of the most distant things a human eye can see unaided. A whole galaxy, looking like a little patch of mist.

Fact sixty five. Andromeda and the Milky Way are moving toward each other. For a long time, astronomers expected them to merge in around four to five billion years. More recent studies suggest it is far less certain, closer to a coin toss over the next ten billion years. Either way, stars would almost never collide. The spaces between them are simply too vast.

Fact sixty six. Galaxies come in many shapes. Some are graceful spirals, with curling arms. Some are smooth, round ellipticals, glowing like soft lanterns. And some are irregular, shaped by close encounters with their neighbours. Each one is a city of stars, floating in the dark.

Fact sixty seven. In nineteen ninety five, astronomers pointed the Hubble Space Telescope at a tiny, empty looking patch of sky for several days. When the image came back, that dark patch was filled with around three thousand galaxies. A speck of sky about the size of a tennis ball seen from a hundred metres away, crowded with distant galaxies.

Fact sixty eight. No one knows exactly how many galaxies there are. Estimates have ranged from hundreds of billions to around two trillion in the observable universe. Each galaxy may contain billions of stars. It is a number best not counted, only gently wondered at.

Fact sixty nine. Most of the matter in the universe cannot be seen. Astronomers call it dark matter. It does not shine, or absorb light, but its gravity holds galaxies together. Without it, many galaxies would spin themselves apart. Something like eighty five percent of all matter may be this invisible substance, quietly holding everything in place.

Fact seventy. The Milky Way belongs to a small group of galaxies called the Local Group. And that group is part of a much larger structure, a supercluster called Laniakea. The name comes from Hawaiian, and is often translated as immeasurable heaven. Which seems about right.

## Light and Time

Fact seventy one. Light travels at about three hundred thousand kilometres per second. In a single second, it could circle the Earth about seven and a half times. It is the fastest thing in the universe. And yet the universe is so large that even light takes millions, and billions, of years to cross it.

Fact seventy two. Looking up is looking back in time. The farther away something is, the longer its light has taken to reach you. The Moon, a little over a second ago. The Sun, eight minutes ago. The stars, years to centuries ago. Every glance at the night sky is a glance into the past.

Fact seventy three. The light we see from the Andromeda Galaxy left it about two and a half million years ago. When it set off, the earliest members of our human family were only beginning to make simple stone tools. That light has been travelling ever since, and arrives tonight, for whoever happens to look up.

Fact seventy four. The oldest light in the universe is all around us. It is called the cosmic microwave background, a faint glow left over from about three hundred and eighty thousand years after the universe began. On old televisions, a small part of the static between channels came from this ancient light. A whisper from the beginning, hidden in the fuzz.

Fact seventy five. The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old. Our Sun and Earth arrived around nine billion years into that story. In cosmic terms, we are quite recent guests. We arrived late to the party, but just in time to notice how beautiful it is.

Fact seventy six. The observable universe, the part whose light has had time to reach us, is about ninety three billion light years across. That is larger than you might expect, because space itself has been stretching while the light was on its way. And beyond what we can observe, the universe may go on much further still.

Fact seventy seven. A light year is a distance, not a time. It is how far light travels in one year, about nine and a half trillion kilometres. Astronomers use it because ordinary kilometres become far too small to be useful out there. Like measuring the distance between cities in grains of rice.

Fact seventy eight. In the nineteen twenties, astronomers discovered that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther away they are, the faster they seem to recede. This was some of the first evidence that the universe is expanding. Not galaxies flying through space, but space itself, slowly stretching between them.

Fact seventy nine. In the late nineteen nineties, astronomers found something even more surprising. The expansion of the universe is speeding up. Whatever is driving it has been named dark energy, and no one yet knows what it is. Most of the universe, it seems, is made of things we are still learning to name.

Fact eighty. Because of that expansion, there are galaxies so far away that their light leaving them now will never reach us. Space between us is growing too quickly. Far, far in the future, the night sky could become a lonelier place. But that is a very, very long way from tonight.

## Strange and Gentle Physics

Fact eighty one. In space, there is no air to carry sound, so space is silent. But in a cluster of galaxies called Perseus, astronomers found pressure waves rippling through hot gas around a giant black hole. If it were a musical note, it would be a B flat, about fifty seven octaves below middle C. A note far too deep for any ear, humming softly in the dark.

Fact eighty two. On the space station, flames burn as round, glowing spheres. On Earth, hot air rises, pulling a flame upward into a teardrop shape. In near weightlessness, there is no up, so a candle flame simply spreads out evenly in every direction. A small, soft ball of blue light.

Fact eighty three. Astronauts grow a little taller in space. Without gravity pressing down, the discs in their spines gently expand. They can gain a few centimetres in height during a long mission. Sadly, it does not last. Gravity takes it back soon after they come home.

Fact eighty four. Astronauts say space has a smell. After spacewalks, they notice a strange scent on their suits and gear, often described as seared steak, hot metal, or welding fumes. Not quite what anyone expected from the heavens. But then the universe has never been very interested in our expectations.

Fact eighty five. Time runs at slightly different speeds in different places. The clocks on GPS satellites tick a tiny bit faster than clocks on the ground, by about thirty eight millionths of a second each day, because of their speed and their height above Earth. Engineers have to correct for this, or the maps on our phones would slowly drift off course.

Fact eighty six. The International Space Station circles the Earth about every ninety minutes. That means the astronauts on board see around sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every single day. A sunrise, every hour and a half. It must be very hard to keep track of breakfast.

Fact eighty seven. Deep inside Uranus and Neptune, it may rain diamonds. Under enormous pressure, carbon in their atmospheres might be squeezed into tiny diamonds that slowly sink toward their cores. Experiments in laboratories on Earth have recreated conditions that support this idea. A soft, sparkling rain, falling for thousands of kilometres.

Fact eighty eight. Space is not completely empty. Between the stars, there are a few atoms in every cubic centimetre. Between galaxies, there may be only about one atom in every cubic metre. Emptier than any vacuum we can make on Earth, but never quite nothing.

Fact eighty nine. Near the centre of our galaxy, there is a huge cloud of gas that contains alcohol. A cloud called Sagittarius B2 holds enormous amounts of ethyl alcohol, the same kind found in drinks, spread very thinly across light years of space. Unfortunately, it is mixed with many other chemicals, and it is a long way to go for a drink.

Fact ninety. We now know of more than six thousand planets orbiting other stars. Some are giant worlds hugging their suns in scorching orbits. Some are rocky, perhaps with oceans. Some orbit two stars at once. Thirty five years ago, we had found none at all. Now we know that planets are everywhere.

## The Quiet Universe

Fact ninety one. Even lying perfectly still tonight, you are moving very fast. The Earth carries you around the Sun at about thirty kilometres every second. Over a full night's sleep, you will travel more than twice the distance from here to the Moon. All without leaving your bed.

Fact ninety two. The Earth is also spinning. At the equator, the ground is moving at about one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres per hour. We do not feel it, because everything around us, the air, the oceans, and our own bodies, is moving along smoothly with it. A great, silent turning, carrying us all toward morning.

Fact ninety three. In nineteen ninety, Voyager 1 turned its camera back toward home, from about six billion kilometres away. In that photograph, the Earth is a tiny, pale blue dot, smaller than a single pixel, floating in a beam of scattered sunlight. Everything anyone has ever known, in one small speck of light.

Fact ninety four. The atoms that make up your body are mostly empty space. Their tiny nuclei sit at the centre of a vast emptiness, surrounded by a haze of electrons. What feels solid to us is really the push of electric forces between atoms. You are, in a sense, a quiet arrangement of almost nothing, held together very kindly.

Fact ninety five. Every day, many tonnes of tiny dust grains from space fall onto the Earth. Most of them are far too small to see, drifting down slowly through the air. Some of the dust on your windowsill tonight may have come from comets and asteroids, after a journey of millions of years.

Fact ninety six. The stars do not go away in the daytime. They are still there, shining as always. The sky simply becomes too bright with scattered sunlight for us to see them. Even now, behind the blue of any afternoon, the stars are waiting patiently for the dark.

Fact ninety seven. Right now, tiny particles called muons are passing harmlessly through your body. They are made high in the atmosphere, when cosmic rays from deep space strike the air. Each one is a tiny visitor, created by a journey that may have begun in an exploding star far away.

Fact ninety eight. The universe is made mostly of hydrogen, the simplest element of all. About three quarters of all the ordinary matter in the cosmos is hydrogen, made in the first few minutes after the beginning. Some of it is in the water in your body. A little piece of the earliest universe, inside you tonight.

Fact ninety nine. The Earth orbits the Sun at just the right distance for liquid water. Not so close that the oceans boil away, and not so far that they freeze solid. Astronomers call this the habitable zone. Our world sits gently inside it, warm enough, cool enough, just right for a quiet night's rest.

Fact one hundred. The astronomer Carl Sagan once said that we are a way for the universe to know itself. Every atom in you came from somewhere out there. And tonight, lying here, listening to these facts, a small part of the universe has been quietly wondering about the rest of it.

Let the wondering fade now. Let your breathing slow. The Sun is shining somewhere. The planets are turning. The galaxies are drifting, far away, in the dark.

There is nothing left to know tonight. Only rest.

Goodnight.
