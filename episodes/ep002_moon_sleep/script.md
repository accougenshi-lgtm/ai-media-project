## The Quiet Neighbour

Tonight, we are going to spend some time with the Moon. There is nothing you need to remember, and nothing you need to do. You can close your eyes, settle into the pillow, and simply let the facts drift past, one after another, like clouds crossing a pale sky.

The Moon is about three thousand, four hundred and seventy four kilometres across. That is a little more than a quarter of the width of the Earth. If the Earth were the size of a basketball, the Moon would be roughly the size of a tennis ball, floating about seven metres away.

On average, it sits around three hundred and eighty four thousand kilometres from us. That sounds like a long way, and it is. But light, the fastest thing there is, makes the journey in a little over one second. When you look up at the Moon, you are seeing it as it was just over a second ago. A tiny, gentle delay.

You could line up every planet in our solar system, side by side, in the gap between the Earth and the Moon. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, all of them, with a little room left over. The space between us is mostly emptiness, and silence.

The Moon has no air to carry sound. If you stood on its surface and clapped your hands, you would feel it, but you would hear nothing. No wind. No birds. No rain. Only stillness, stretching out in every direction, under a black sky full of stars.

It is the fifth largest moon in the solar system. Jupiter and Saturn have bigger ones. But compared to the size of the planet it circles, our Moon is unusually large. It is less like a small companion, and more like a partner, the two of us turning together through the dark.

And it has been there, every single night of human history. Over every campfire. Over every sleeping village. Over everyone who has ever looked up and wondered what it was.

## A Collision, Long Ago

Most scientists think the Moon was born in a collision.

About four and a half billion years ago, when the Earth was still young and hot, another world, perhaps about the size of Mars, drifted into its path. Scientists sometimes call this lost world Theia, after a figure from Greek mythology, the mother of the Moon goddess Selene.

The impact would have been enormous, but slow, in the way that very large things are slow. Rock melted. Part of the outer layers of both worlds was thrown out into space, forming a glowing ring of debris around the Earth.

Over time, perhaps surprisingly quickly, that debris gathered together. Grain by grain, piece by piece, gravity pulled it into a single sphere. And the Moon was there.

One reason this idea is so widely accepted comes from the rocks that astronauts brought back. When scientists studied them, they found that the Moon's rocks are remarkably similar to Earth's in their chemistry, especially in the forms of oxygen they contain. It is as if the Moon is made, in large part, from the same family of material as our own planet.

The Moon is also short of iron compared to the Earth. Its core is small. That fits the story too. The heavy iron of the young Earth had already sunk toward the centre, so the material thrown outward was mostly the lighter, rocky outer layers.

There is still a great deal we do not know. Scientists continue to debate the details, the size of the impactor, the angle, the timing. New ideas are still being explored. But the broad picture has held for decades: the Moon may be, in a sense, a piece of the young Earth, given back to the sky.

So when you look at it tonight, you might think of it this way. Not a stranger. Something closer to a sibling. Made, long ago, from the same ancient stone.

## The Face That Never Turns Away

Every person who has ever lived has seen the same side of the Moon.

The patterns you know, the dark patches, the bright rays, have looked down on Earth for as long as there have been eyes to see them. The Moon does spin, but it spins exactly once for every trip it makes around us. Its day and its orbit match. And so the same face is always turned toward the Earth.

This is called tidal locking. Long ago, the Moon probably turned more quickly. But the Earth's gravity pulled very slightly on the Moon, stretching it, just a little, into an egg-like shape. Over a very long time, that gentle pull slowed its spin, until the long side settled, facing us, and stayed.

The Moon takes about twenty seven days to complete one orbit, measured against the distant stars. And in that same time, it turns once on its axis. Slow and steady, like a dancer who keeps their eyes on their partner while circling the floor.

But the dance is not perfectly precise. The Moon's orbit is slightly oval, and slightly tilted. Because of this, it seems to rock, very gently, nodding and turning a little from side to side over the month. This slow wobble is called libration.

Thanks to libration, we can see a little more than half of the Moon from Earth over time. About fifty nine percent, in total. Just a little peek around the edges.

The far side, the part we never see from here, is sometimes called the dark side. But it is not dark at all. It receives just as much sunlight as the near side. It simply faces away from us.

It was not until 1959 that anyone saw it, when a Soviet spacecraft sent back the first, grainy photographs. And the far side turned out to look quite different. Fewer dark plains. More craters. A rougher, older landscape, quietly keeping to itself.

## Phases of Silver Light

The Moon does not make its own light. Everything you see when you look at it is sunlight, reflected back toward you.

As the Moon travels around the Earth, the angle between the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon slowly changes. And so the part of the Moon we see lit up changes too. A thin crescent. A half. A swelling gibbous Moon. Then full. And then back again, slowly fading.

From one new Moon to the next takes about twenty nine and a half days. That is a little longer than the Moon's trip around the Earth, because while the Moon circles us, we are also moving around the Sun. The Moon has to travel a little extra to catch up to the same alignment.

This rhythm has shaped human life for thousands of years. Many calendars were built around it. Even the word month comes from the same ancient root as the word moon.

At new Moon, the side facing us is in shadow, and the Moon is nearly invisible, lost in the glare of the Sun during the day. At full Moon, it sits opposite the Sun. It rises as the Sun sets, and it sets as the Sun rises. It keeps watch through the whole night.

Sometimes, when the Moon is a thin crescent, you can faintly see the rest of its disc, glowing a soft, ashen grey. This is called earthshine. Sunlight bounces off the Earth, travels to the Moon, and then reflects back to us again. Light, taking the long way home.

More than five hundred years ago, Leonardo da Vinci wrote about this glow in his notebooks, and he correctly guessed where it came from. Light from our own world, softly lighting up the night side of the Moon.

So on a quiet evening, when you see that faint grey disc cradled in a sliver of silver, you are seeing the Earth's own light, reflected back. A little glow from home.

## Seas Without Water

When people first looked closely at the Moon, they saw dark patches and imagined they were seas.

The name stuck. Astronomers still call them maria, which is Latin for seas. There is the Sea of Tranquillity, the Sea of Serenity, the Sea of Rains, the Ocean of Storms. Beautiful names, for places with no water at all.

The maria are really vast plains of dark volcanic rock, a type of rock called basalt. Billions of years ago, molten rock welled up from inside the Moon and flooded huge, low basins, many of them made by ancient impacts. It cooled slowly, and hardened into smooth, dark plains.

The brighter regions are called the highlands. They are older, rougher, and covered in craters. Their pale colour comes from a light, mineral rich rock that may have floated to the top of an ocean of molten rock when the Moon was very young, like cream rising in milk.

Craters are everywhere. Some are tiny, smaller than a grain of sand. Some are hundreds of kilometres across. On Earth, craters are slowly erased by wind, rain, plants, and moving rock. On the Moon, there is almost nothing to wear them away. And so the history of countless impacts is kept, layer upon layer.

One of the brightest craters is called Tycho. On a full Moon, you can see long, pale streaks spreading out from it across the surface, like the spokes of a wheel. These are rays of material thrown out when it formed, thought to be roughly a hundred million years ago. Young, by lunar standards.

The ground itself is covered in a fine grey dust and broken rock, called regolith, built up by billions of years of tiny impacts. Astronauts described it as soft, powdery, and clinging to everything.

And because there is no wind, the footprints they left behind are probably still there. Perfectly still. Waiting in the quiet grey dust.

## Heat, Cold, and Hidden Ice

A day on the Moon is long. From one sunrise to the next takes about twenty nine and a half Earth days. Two weeks of light. Two weeks of darkness.

With no air to hold heat, or spread it around, the temperatures swing to extremes. Near the equator, in the long afternoon, the ground can reach over one hundred degrees Celsius, hotter than boiling water. And in the long night, it can fall below minus one hundred and thirty degrees.

But there are places on the Moon that never see the Sun at all.

Near the north and south poles, the Sun always sits low on the horizon. Some craters there are deep enough that their floors have stayed in shadow for perhaps billions of years. These are called permanently shadowed regions.

Inside them, it is among the coldest places ever measured in our solar system. Colder than the surface of Pluto, in some spots. Temperatures can drop to around minus two hundred and forty degrees Celsius.

In that endless cold, something precious may have collected: water ice.

In 2009, a spacecraft was deliberately sent crashing into one of these shadowed craters near the south pole. A second spacecraft, flying behind it, watched the plume of material that was thrown up, and found the signature of water.

Since then, other missions have found more evidence of ice hidden in the shadows, and traces of water bound into the soil in other places too. Not lakes, and not seas. But frost and ice, mixed into the cold, dark ground.

For future explorers, this matters a great deal. Water can be used for drinking, and it can be split into oxygen to breathe, and into hydrogen for fuel. Ancient ice, kept frozen in the dark, might one day help people live on the Moon.

So in the deepest shadows of the Moon, where no sunlight has fallen for ages, there may be a quiet treasure. Waiting, perfectly still, in the cold.

## The Slow Goodbye

The Moon is slowly drifting away from us.

Each year, it moves about three point eight centimetres farther from the Earth. That is roughly the speed at which your fingernails grow. Too slow to notice in a lifetime. But over millions of years, it adds up.

We know this so precisely because of a few small mirrors, left on the Moon's surface. The Apollo astronauts placed some of them there, and robotic Soviet rovers carried others. They are called retroreflectors. Special mirrors that send light straight back in the direction it came from.

On Earth, observatories fire laser pulses at these mirrors. The light travels to the Moon, bounces back, and returns, two and a half seconds later. By timing that journey extremely carefully, scientists can measure the distance to the Moon to within a few millimetres.

So why is the Moon moving away?

It is because of the tides. The Moon's gravity raises gentle bulges in Earth's oceans. As the Earth spins, it carries those bulges slightly ahead of the Moon. And those bulges pull forward on the Moon, just a little, giving it a tiny push into a higher orbit.

At the same time, the friction of the tides is slowing the Earth's spin. Very, very gradually, our days are getting longer. By about two thousandths of a second every century.

Long ago, the days were shorter. Studies of ancient rocks and fossil corals suggest that hundreds of millions of years ago, a year had more days in it than it does today, because each day was shorter.

The Earth and the Moon are still slowly adjusting to one another. A dance that began billions of years ago, still continuing tonight. The Moon is not leaving in any hurry. It will be keeping us company for a very, very long time to come.

## Footprints in the Dust

Only twelve people have ever walked on the Moon.

They went between 1969 and 1972, during the Apollo programme. Six landings. Twelve astronauts stepping out onto the grey ground, while millions of people on Earth listened and watched.

The first landing came in July 1969. Apollo 11 settled down on the Sea of Tranquillity. The lunar module was called Eagle. And the first steps were taken a few hours later, slowly, carefully, onto the soft, powdery dust.

The astronauts found that moving in the Moon's low gravity took some getting used to. Many of them found that a kind of bouncing, hopping stride worked better than walking. Their suits were bulky, and the dust clung to everything.

On the later missions, they brought a small electric car, the lunar roving vehicle, so they could travel farther from their landers, out across the plains and up to the edges of mountains and craters.

Across all six landings, they gathered and brought home about three hundred and eighty two kilograms of lunar rock and soil. Scientists around the world are still studying those samples today, more than fifty years later, using tools that did not exist when they were collected.

The last astronauts to walk on the Moon left in December 1972, at the end of Apollo 17. Before they left, they looked back at the landscape around them, a quiet valley surrounded by mountains, and then climbed back inside, and began the long journey home.

Since then, no one has returned. But the things they left behind are still there. Flags, bleached by decades of harsh sunlight. Scientific instruments. Tools. And the footprints. Thousands of them, scattered across the grey ground, perfectly preserved in the stillness.

## Lighter Steps

On the Moon, you would weigh about one sixth of what you weigh on Earth.

The Moon has much less mass than our planet, so its pull is gentler. A step becomes a glide. A jump lifts you higher, and brings you down more slowly. Everything falls, but it falls softly.

On the Apollo 15 mission, one of the astronauts held up a hammer and a feather, and let them go at the same time. With no air to slow the feather down, the two fell side by side, and touched the ground together. A simple demonstration of an idea Galileo had described centuries earlier.

The Moon is not completely silent inside, though. It has moonquakes.

The Apollo astronauts left seismometers on the surface, instruments that recorded the Moon's tremors for years. Some quakes come from deep inside, triggered by the Earth's gravity gently squeezing the Moon as it moves along its orbit. Others are shallower, and some are caused by meteorites striking the surface.

The Moon behaves differently from the Earth when it is shaken. Its rock is dry and cold, and it carries vibrations for a long time. When part of a used spacecraft was deliberately crashed onto the surface during Apollo 12, the instruments recorded the Moon trembling for close to an hour. Scientists described it as ringing.

Imagine it. The whole Moon, quietly ringing, long and low, like a bell struck once in a silent hall.

Its gravity also shapes life on Earth. The rhythm of the tides, rising and falling twice a day along every coastline, follows the Moon. Creatures in rock pools, birds on the shore, and coral on the reef all live by that rhythm, set, in part, by the Moon.

So even this far away, the Moon is reaching gently across the space between us, tugging on the oceans, and keeping time.

## A Perfect Coincidence

The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon. And by a lovely coincidence, it is also about four hundred times farther away.

Because of this, from the Earth, the Sun and the Moon appear almost exactly the same size in the sky. And that is why we can have total solar eclipses.

During a total eclipse, the Moon slides perfectly in front of the Sun. The sky darkens in the middle of the day. The temperature drops. Birds fall quiet. And around the black disc of the Moon, the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, appears as a soft, pearly glow stretching out into space.

It only happens along a narrow path on Earth's surface, and the darkness lasts only a few minutes at most. People travel across the world to stand in that shadow, just for a moment.

Because the Moon's orbit is slightly oval, sometimes it is a little farther away, and looks a little smaller. Then it cannot quite cover the Sun, and a bright ring of sunlight remains around it. This is called an annular eclipse.

There are lunar eclipses too, when the Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon. The Earth's shadow falls across the Moon. But it does not disappear. Instead, it often turns a deep, coppery red.

That red glow is sunlight that has passed through the edges of Earth's atmosphere, bent and filtered on the way. In a sense, the Moon is lit by every sunrise and every sunset happening around the Earth at that moment, all at once.

And this perfect match in size will not last forever. Since the Moon is slowly moving away, it will one day look too small to fully cover the Sun. Far in the future, total solar eclipses will come to an end. For now, we happen to live in just the right time to see them.

## Return to the Moon

After a long pause, people are preparing to return to the Moon.

Space agencies and companies from several countries are building new spacecraft, new landers, and new rovers. Robotic missions have already touched down in recent years, some near the poles, some even on the far side.

Many of these efforts are focused on the region around the south pole. It is a place of long shadows and low sunlight, where the edges of some craters stay lit for much of the time, while their floors stay in darkness. Light for solar power, and cold shadows that may hold ice. A combination that could be very useful for anyone hoping to stay a while.

There are ideas for small bases, built from materials sent from Earth, and perhaps one day from the lunar soil itself. Scientists have experimented with ways to turn regolith into bricks, or to shield shelters with it, protecting people from radiation and from tiny meteorites.

Radio astronomers dream of the far side, too. Shielded from the radio noise of Earth by the Moon itself, it could be one of the quietest places in the inner solar system to listen to the universe.

No one knows exactly how the next chapter will unfold. Plans change. Missions are delayed. New discoveries change what people want to do. Exploration has always been like that. Slow, patient, and full of surprises.

But the Moon does not mind waiting. It has waited billions of years already. Turning slowly, keeping the same face toward us, collecting starlight, and frost, and quiet.

## Drifting Off

Let us end where we began. With the Moon, high and pale, in a quiet sky.

Three thousand, four hundred and seventy four kilometres across. Three hundred and eighty four thousand kilometres away. A world of grey dust and ancient stone, perhaps made from the same material as the Earth beneath you.

Its seas are made of old lava. Its shadows may hold ice older than any memory. Its footprints have stayed untouched for more than fifty years. And every year, it drifts a few centimetres farther, as patient as anything has ever been.

Right now, the Moon is somewhere in its orbit. Perhaps a crescent, perhaps full, perhaps hidden in the glow of the Sun. But it is there. Above the clouds. Above the rooftops. Moving quietly along the path it has followed for billions of years.

You do not need to watch it. You do not need to remember any of this. You can simply let the thoughts slow down, and soften, and fade.

Let your breathing settle. Let your shoulders sink. Let the weight of the day drift away, like a crescent Moon sinking slowly toward the horizon.

The tides will keep rising and falling. The Moon will keep turning. The stars will keep shining, far away, in the dark.

And you can rest now.

Goodnight.
